# Technical claim and citation audit

**Date:** 5 November 2026  
**Manuscript:** *LLM for OS Security: A Retrospective Survey*

## Review process

Three parallel, section-focused subagents reviewed (1) kernel and drivers, (2) file systems, networking, and services, and (3) the introduction, background, methodology, and virtualization/TEE sections. An independent OpenCode run using deepseek/deepseek-flash reviewed the claim-to-source alignment. Reviews used abstracts and conclusions for initial scope checks, then publisher, conference, author, or full-paper sources where a number, target, or result needed confirmation.

The current citation-mapping script scans active citations in the manuscript and updated representative-study table. It found 116 cited BibTeX keys, no missing BibTeX entries, 34 links to the 225-record candidate inventory, and 24 links to the saved discovery snapshot. Those mappings identify records; they do not certify the technical claim attached to a citation.

## Corrections made

| Manuscript area | Claim/source issue found | Disposition |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction and background | DR.CHECKER was described as generic evidence about noisy analysis. Its study reports 3,973 warnings at 78% precision across nine mobile kernels. Scaling, few-shot prompting, and instruction-following evidence also came from distinct studies. | Narrowed the warning claim to the measured result and separated model-capability claims. |
| Introduction and kernel analysis | LLIFT was mischaracterized as explaining analysis outputs. Its evaluated target is Linux-kernel use-before-initialization (UBI) detection; the paper reports four previously undiscovered UBI bugs acknowledged by the Linux community. | Rewrote the description around UBI static-analysis augmentation and the reported result. [Primary paper](https://doi.org/10.1145/3649828). |
| Kernel formal verification | General Python-to-Lean benchmarks, a Coq neural theorem prover, JavaScript property-access analysis, and a research agenda were presented beside kernel-specific proof work as though they showed kernel verification outcomes. | Separated seL4-specific evidence from transferable methods; identified GPass as a non-LLM theorem prover, ABSINT-AI as JavaScript-only, VeriBench as Python-to-Lean, and the Unit Proofing Framework as a research agenda. |
| Kernel analysis | BugStone was described too narrowly as direct LLVM integration, while several general-purpose analysis and fuzzing tools were described as kernel results. | Described BugStone as combining LLVM-based analysis with LLM-guided reasoning; labeled GVI, Fuzz4All, RandLuzz, HLPFuzz, and related methods by their evaluated targets. [PMLR paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/wu26bk.html). |
| Kernel bug triage | BugLens's four reported vulnerabilities were described as new without the paper's manual-review context. KNighter's 32.2% false-positive rate could be read as a general rate. | The comparison table states that BugLens recovered four true bugs previously dismissed in Suture's manual review. KNighter's rate is explicitly scoped to the paper's evaluated triage reports. [BugLens paper](https://doi.org/10.1109/ASE63991.2025.00039), [KNighter paper](https://yangchenyuan.github.io/files/SOSP25-KNighter.pdf). |
| Kernel patching | Ksplice was used to support a broad list of concurrency, memory-ordering, ABI, and performance constraints; kAgent's tool feedback could be read as proof of correctness. | Reframed the evidence around live update and patch migration. Identified kAgent (formerly circulated as CrashFixer) as producing candidate patches whose build and reproducer checks do not establish semantic correctness. [kAgent v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20412). |
| Device drivers | Static analysis, symbolic execution, fuzzing, and driver-environment modeling were blended into one claim. | Separated Ball et al.'s static-analysis modeling, SymDrive's path/environment challenges, and DiFuze's interface-aware fuzzing. Removed the unsupported blanket precision-versus-coverage claim. |
| Query synthesis | QLCoder could be read as kernel-specific evidence because it appears in the kernel-analysis discussion. | The cited ICLR 2026 paper evaluates query synthesis on 176 CVEs across 111 Java projects; 53.4% meet its vulnerable-versus-patched success criterion. The manuscript now labels it as transferable and says kernel applicability requires separate evaluation. AEM and SparSamp occur only in commented-out legacy text, so neither makes an active technical claim. [ICLR paper](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/2adf01ab15adde8820622f7f24bd516b-Abstract-Conference.html), [full paper](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=J91IKwJrqv). |
| File systems | luAAL14 pointed to Cai et al.'s 2019 security-vulnerability study even though the key and introduction referred to Lu et al.'s 2014 file-system evolution paper. | Restored luAAL14 to Lu et al. and added cai2019understanding for the 157-CVE security study. [Lu et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/2560012), [Cai et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/3343737.3343753). |
| File systems | PathSentinel was described too broadly; SysGPT and SysSpec were at risk of being read as security-evaluated file-system models. | Scoped PathSentinel to filesystem-access findings in Android applications/core services, with the LLM generating validation tests. Identified SysGPT as a serial-performance model with a file/storage case study and SysSpec as a functional file-system generation/evolution study, not a security evaluation. [PathSentinel](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11279), [SysSpec](https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast26/presentation/liu-qingyuan). |
| Networking | ChatAFL and other protocol studies could be read as evaluations of the OS network stack. PARVAL's single BFD-parser case could be generalized to multiple protocols; HDHunter was not clearly distinguished as a non-LLM comparator. | Marked the primary LLM protocol-fuzzing targets as application protocols or IoT devices; narrowed PARVAL to its FRRouting BFD-parser case study; labeled HDHunter as non-LLM HTTP-desynchronization work. Removed the unrelated Trace/network-telemetry claim. [ChatAFL](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/ndss-paper/large-language-model-guided-protocol-fuzzing/), [PARVAL](https://langsechq.gitlab.io/spw25/papers/zheng-llm-parser-validation.pdf), [HDHunter](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/mu). |
| System services | HexT5, LogLLM, LWDIFF, LLMSAN, Clarify, template-guided repair, and Salva et al. were at risk of being presented as evaluations of privileged OS services. | Labeled their actual targets: decompiled binaries, general software logs, WebAssembly, LLM-assisted bug-detection paths, network ACLs, general repair benchmarks, and RESTful applications. Added the need for separate evaluation on privileged OS services. [LogLLM](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/11720227), [Salva et al.](https://www.scitepress.org/PublicationsDetail.aspx?ID=fWNC7aU7+3A%3D&t=1). |
| Hypervisors and TEEs | Nyx/HYPERPILL, TEE fuzzing, enclave/bootloader analysis, and TEE-backed LLM inference were grouped as if they tested one target. Direct LLM work on TEE-boundary design was absent. | Separated hypervisor fuzzing, TEE interface testing, enclave analysis, and adjacent model protection. Added LLM-CAL, which labels cryptographic code for TEE placement; the manuscript states that label metrics do not prove the security of a resulting partition. The detailed results table reports 98.41% line-level F1 and 97.50% recall; the paper abstract rounds F1 to 98.40%. [NDSS page](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/ndss-paper/automated-code-annotation-with-llms-for-establishing-tee-boundaries/), [paper PDF](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2026-s709-paper.pdf). |
